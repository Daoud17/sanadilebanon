Mental Health & Wellbeing Resources

What Is Mental Health?

Health includes physical, mental, and social wellbeing. Mental health is an essential part of overall health and influences how we think, feel, relate to others, and respond to everyday challenges.

Just as we care for our physical health, it is equally important to understand, protect, and support our mental and emotional wellbeing.

Mental health can affect our emotions, thoughts, behaviors, relationships, decision-making, and ability to function in daily life.

Good mental wellbeing does not mean never experiencing difficulties. It means having access to support, healthy coping strategies, and the resources needed to navigate challenges with dignity and resilience.

Mental Health Information

Resources

Explore general information about common mental health conditions, suicide prevention, grief, and ways to support someone who may be struggling.

Anxiety is a normal response to stress and uncertainty, but anxiety disorders involve fear or worry that can become persistent, difficult to control, and disruptive to everyday activities. A person may experience excessive worrying, physical tension, rapid heartbeat, difficulty concentrating, sleep disturbance, avoidance, or a persistent feeling that something bad may happen.

Anxiety disorders can take different forms, including generalized anxiety disorder, panic disorder, social anxiety, specific phobias, and other anxiety-related conditions. Symptoms and their intensity can vary significantly from one person to another.

Support may include psychotherapy, lifestyle changes, coping strategies, stress-management techniques, and, when appropriate, medical treatment. Early support can help individuals better understand their symptoms and reduce the effect anxiety has on daily life.

Eating disorders are serious mental health conditions that affect a person's relationship with food, eating, weight, and body image. They are not simply about food or appearance; they can involve complex emotional, psychological, social, and physical factors.

Common eating disorders include anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge-eating disorder, and other specified feeding or eating disorders. Warning signs may include restrictive eating, episodes of overeating, compensatory behaviors, intense concern about weight or appearance, secrecy around food, or significant changes in eating habits.

Eating disorders can affect people of different ages, genders, and backgrounds. Professional support may involve psychological care, nutritional guidance, medical monitoring, and support from family or trusted people.

Mood disorders are mental health conditions that primarily affect a person's emotional state. They may involve prolonged periods of sadness, hopelessness, low motivation, elevated mood, irritability, or significant changes in energy and activity.

Depression and bipolar disorders are among the better-known mood disorders. Symptoms may influence sleep, appetite, concentration, relationships, work, studies, and a person's ability to enjoy activities they previously valued.

Treatment and support depend on the individual and may include psychotherapy, medication, lifestyle adjustments, social support, and regular follow-up with a qualified mental health professional.

Obsessive-compulsive disorder, commonly known as OCD, involves recurring unwanted thoughts, images, or urges called obsessions, as well as repetitive behaviors or mental acts known as compulsions.

A person may feel driven to perform certain actions in order to reduce distress or prevent a feared outcome. Examples can include repeated checking, cleaning, counting, reassurance seeking, or mental rituals.

OCD can become time-consuming and interfere with daily life, relationships, work, or education. Evidence-based treatments, including specific forms of psychotherapy and medication, can help many people manage symptoms effectively.

Psychotic disorders can affect the way a person interprets reality. Symptoms may include hallucinations, delusions, confused thinking, unusual beliefs, changes in behavior, or difficulty organizing thoughts and communication.

Psychosis can occur in several mental health conditions and may also be associated with physical illness, substance use, or other factors. The experience can be distressing for both the individual and those close to them.

Early professional assessment is important. Treatment can involve medication, psychotherapy, family support, rehabilitation, and ongoing community-based care depending on the person's needs.

The way suicide is reported in the media can influence public understanding, stigma, and help-seeking. Responsible reporting aims to inform audiences without sensationalizing suicide or presenting it as an inevitable response to hardship.

Journalists are encouraged to use respectful language, avoid unnecessary details about methods, provide context, include information about available support, and highlight stories of recovery, prevention, and people seeking help.

Sensitive reporting can contribute to suicide prevention by challenging stigma, encouraging conversations about mental health, and directing people experiencing distress toward appropriate support.

Suicide is a complex public health and mental health issue that can be influenced by psychological, social, medical, environmental, and personal factors. There is rarely one single reason behind suicidal thoughts or behavior.

Warning signs can include expressions of hopelessness, withdrawal, major changes in mood or behavior, giving away possessions, increased substance use, talking about death, or saying that others would be better off without them.

Open conversations, compassionate listening, early intervention, professional support, and reducing access to immediate means of harm can all play an important role in prevention.

Losing someone to suicide can bring an intense and complicated grieving process. People may experience sadness, shock, anger, guilt, confusion, unanswered questions, or a sense of isolation.

Grief does not follow a fixed timeline, and different people may respond in very different ways. Some may need space, while others may benefit from speaking with family, friends, mental health professionals, or people who have experienced a similar loss.

Seeking support after suicide loss can help people process difficult emotions, reduce isolation, and find ways to remember their loved one while gradually rebuilding stability in their own lives.

If someone you know appears overwhelmed, withdrawn, unusually distressed, hopeless, or significantly different from their usual behavior, reaching out can make an important difference.

Try to speak calmly, listen without judgment, take their concerns seriously, and avoid immediately trying to solve everything. Asking directly about how they are feeling can help create space for an honest conversation.

If you believe the person may be at immediate risk of harming themselves or someone else, do not leave them alone and seek urgent professional or emergency support. Supporting someone does not mean managing the situation alone.

Research, Reports & Insights

Publications

Explore research, reports, policy briefs, service updates, and field insights related to mental health, suicide prevention, emergency response, and community-based care in Lebanon.

Flash Report

National Mobile Crisis Team (MCT) Flash Report No.1: Caseload & Coverage

This flash report provides an overview of the National Mobile Crisis Team’s activities, including caseload patterns, service reach, geographic coverage, and the types of mental health needs addressed within communities.

It also highlights the role of mobile mental health services in responding to urgent psychological and psychiatric needs, particularly for individuals who may face barriers to reaching traditional health facilities.

Innovation

Commitment to Build MENA’s First AI-Powered Suicide Prevention Hotline

This initiative explores how artificial intelligence can support suicide prevention services and strengthen the capacity of crisis-response systems across the region.

The project aims to examine how technology can complement human support by improving accessibility, response capacity, service organization, and the ability to identify emerging patterns of psychological distress.

Research

Burnout Indicators Among Operators of Lebanon’s National Suicide Prevention Lifeline During Wartime

This research examines occupational strain, emotional exhaustion, and burnout among professionals and volunteers providing crisis-line support during periods of conflict.

It considers the psychological demands associated with repeated exposure to distress, high call volumes, crisis situations, and the responsibility of supporting individuals experiencing emotional emergencies.

Community Care

Bringing Mental Health Care Closer to Communities Across Lebanon

This publication reflects on efforts to expand mental health services beyond traditional clinical settings and make support more accessible within local communities.

It highlights the importance of outreach, community-based interventions, early support, and reducing geographic, financial, and social barriers that can prevent individuals from accessing mental health care.

Emergency Response

Critical Mental Health Support in Lebanon

This report documents mental health and psychosocial support delivered during a period of increased national crisis and community distress.

It provides insight into emerging mental health needs, service demand, outreach activities, emergency interventions, and the challenges involved in maintaining continuity of care during unstable conditions.

Lifeline Report

National Lifeline Flash Report: Caller Trends & Service Reach

This report presents general patterns observed among individuals contacting a national emotional support and suicide prevention service.

It examines areas such as reasons for calling, emotional distress, patterns of help-seeking, service utilization, and the broader reach of crisis-support services among different communities.

Crisis Response

Critical Mental Health Support Amid Ongoing Conflict

This publication examines the psychological impact of prolonged instability and the resulting demand for mental health and psychosocial support.

It describes how crisis services adapt to changing community needs, including increased distress, displacement, fear, uncertainty, grief, and difficulties accessing regular mental health services.

Data Analysis

National Lifeline Flash Report: Wartime Call Trends

This analysis looks at how crisis conditions can influence call patterns, emotional distress, help-seeking behavior, and the types of concerns presented to a national lifeline.

Comparing different periods of crisis can help identify changing mental health needs and support service planning, preparedness, resource allocation, and emergency response.

Emergency Update

Mental Health Emergency Response During War

This emergency update outlines the psychological needs that can emerge rapidly during war, including fear, anxiety, grief, displacement-related distress, and uncertainty.

It also describes the importance of rapidly mobilizing crisis support, referral pathways, community outreach, psychological first aid, and accessible mental health information during emergency situations.

Research

Factors Associated with Suicidal Ideation and Behaviour: Analysis from the National Helpline

This research explores factors that may be associated with suicidal thoughts and behaviors among individuals reaching out to a national support service.

The analysis may help improve understanding of psychological, social, behavioral, and situational factors linked to suicide risk and contribute to more informed prevention and intervention strategies.

Clinical Practice

Adapting Psychiatric Practice in War Zones: Clinicians’ Experience from Lebanon

This publication explores how mental health professionals adapt psychiatric practice while working under conditions of conflict, insecurity, and disruption.

It considers challenges related to assessment, treatment, continuity of care, medication access, communication, displacement, and the changing needs of patients experiencing ongoing psychological stress.

Service Delivery

Mental Health Needs and Service Delivery in the Post-War Period

This publication examines mental health needs that may continue after an acute period of conflict has ended.

It highlights the importance of sustained services for individuals experiencing trauma, grief, anxiety, depression, displacement-related difficulties, and other psychological consequences that may remain long after immediate danger has decreased.

Emergency Response

This policy brief focuses on the importance of responsible, sensitive, and evidence-informed reporting when covering suicide and suicide-related stories.
